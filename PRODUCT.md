# Fix the learning, protect the learner

The student loses most when a question is broken: time, marks, and trust in their own understanding. A student preparing under pressure should not need to argue persuasively to get a fair check. The console groups repeated reports, prioritizes scoring and answerability risks, and gives faculty a readable question, exact diff, concise evidence, and a supportive reply in one place. Several reports increase urgency, not mathematical certainty.

I would never let AI invent a missing diagram or original distractor, declare syllabus exclusions, move subject/chapter metadata through an unauthorized patch, or force a key change to agree with a faulty solution. Taxonomy permissions are a ceiling. Missing support and disputed assessment types still go to faculty even when the taxonomy permits automation. Answer-key corrections need independent reasoning; structural validation alone is insufficient.

My week-one metric is **faculty-confirmed harmful `auto_apply` proposals / audited `auto_apply` proposals**, reporting both counts. I would audit a sample of ordinary proposals, not only cases that attract another complaint. This measures the current system's recommendations; no production edits are applied by this demo. A small denominator is not proof of safety, and a fast queue cannot excuse teaching an incorrect result.

Beyond this brief, I would pitch a **correction propagation workflow**. When faculty verify a scoring defect, identify affected attempts, prepare a plain-language correction, and let faculty approve reassessment or learner follow-up. Fixing the database does not repair the harm to a student who already encountered the question.

The console discloses whether its precomputed findings came from the assistant-reviewed baseline or a completed live provider run. The UI shows evidence and guarded edits without claiming hidden-ground-truth accuracy. Demo approvals change an isolated workspace and send no student messages.
